The landscape

Two kinds of essays you'll write

📄 Personal Statement

  • One essay, ~650 words
  • Goes to every school on the Common App
  • Your big-picture story & voice

🧩 Supplemental Essays

  • School-specific, often 100–400 words
  • "Why us?", community, leadership, diversity
  • Show fit + reasons for this school

We'll master the personal statement first, then tackle supplements.

Part 1 · The Common App essay

Breaking down the prompts

Background 🌱

An identity, interest, or talent so meaningful your app would be incomplete without it.

Challenge 💪

A setback, failure, or obstacle — and what you learned from it.

Belief 🤔

A time you questioned or challenged an idea. What prompted it?

Gratitude 🙏

Something someone did that made you happy or grateful, and how it changed you.

Growth 🚀

An accomplishment or realization that sparked personal growth.

Your choice ✨

Any topic of your choice — or an essay you've already written.

Pick the prompt your story already fits — don't force a story to match a prompt.

Mindset

The essay is not a résumé

Your grades and activities are already on the application. The essay's only job is to make an admissions officer feel who you are — and that comes from a story, told in your voice.

  • 650 words, one human, one voice.
  • They read thousands of essays — yours stands out by being specific.
  • Show growth, not perfection.
📚 Guest wisdom · Harvard College

7 (very unofficial)
tips for your essay

Straight from a Harvard student's honest, funny advice — the same rules the pros use.

Source: "My Very Unofficial Tips for Writing Your College Essay" · college.harvard.edu

1
Harvard tip

Make it uniquely you

The personal essay is the only place admissions officers hear your actual voice. If someone dropped 1,000 nameless essays on the floor, your friends and family should be able to pick out yours.

  • Write in your regular, everyday voice — not "English-paper formal."
  • This isn't an essay on Hamlet. It's an essay on you.
  • The instinct to sound "proper and elevated" is baloney. Sound human.
2
Harvard tip

Pick a topic that matters to you

It doesn't have to be sad, dramatic, or impressive. You can write about your mom, a trip to McDonald's with friends, or anything — as long as it brings out your personality.

  • Don't try to "impress" — they already see your resume.
  • Brainstorm a ton of ideas, then choose the one that's most you.
  • Pick something you won't get bored of — you'll live with it a while.
3
Harvard tips 3 & 4

Just start writing — and write a lot

Nothing is worse than a blank screen. Once words are down, more ideas come. Pour everything out about your topic — even the messy parts — then whittle the novel down to 650 words.

  • A stinky first draft still gives your essay a shape.
  • For every sentence that makes the cut, ~4 won't. That's normal — trim the fat.
  • Overwrite first, edit second. You can't edit a blank page.
5
Harvard tip

Rewrite, rewrite, rewrite

The Great Gatsby wasn't written in one draft. Neither was the Declaration of Independence. Masterpieces get revised — a lot.

  • Read it out loud. Sounds weird? Fix it. Boring? Jazz it up.
  • Unlike a class paper, strangers who don't know you will read this.
  • Keep rewriting until you're proud of it — or even before you are.
6
Harvard tip

Show it to other people

Let your mom read it. Show your teacher. Show the milkman if you have one. Fresh eyes make every essay stronger — you just have to get past the awkwardness.

  • Advice that rings true? Great, use it.
  • Advice you disagree with? Great, you don't have to take it.
  • More perspectives = a stronger essay. Every time.
7
Harvard tip · the finish line

Are you proud of it?

If no → keep rewriting, or start fresh with a new topic. Sometimes an essay is like overcooked cookies — frosting won't save it, and starting over is totally fine.

  • Don't get stuck in negativity, and don't freak out.
  • If yes → well done. You've written your college essay. 🎉
The big trap

Authentic vs. what you think they want

❌ Writing to impress

  • Big words you'd never say out loud
  • A "perfect," conflict-free hero
  • The topic you think sounds impressive
  • Generic lessons ("I learned teamwork")

✓ Writing authentically

  • Your real voice and vocabulary
  • Honest struggle + real growth
  • The topic that's genuinely you
  • Specific, personal takeaways

Admissions officers read thousands of "impressive" essays. Authenticity is what they remember.

From the webinar

What makes an essay stand out

  • Start strong — open in the middle of a moment, not with a thesis statement.
  • Show, don't tell — details, dialogue, and senses beat adjectives.
  • Stay focused — one moment, one idea, done well.
  • Sound authentic — your voice is your advantage.
  • Reflect — the "so what?" matters as much as the story.
  • Connect to the future — hint at who you'll be on campus.
Editing strategies

How to polish a draft

Pass 1 · Structure
Does it flow?
Strong opening, clear middle, reflective ending. Cut anything that doesn't earn its place.
Pass 2 · Voice
Read it aloud
If you'd never say it out loud, rewrite it. Trim big words and clichés.
Pass 3 · Details
Show, don't tell
Swap "I was nervous" for the shaking hands. Add one vivid, specific image.
Pass 4 · Length
Hit 650
Cut ~4 sentences for every keeper. Every word should earn its spot.
Pass 5 · Fresh eyes
Get feedback
Give it to a teacher, peer, or family member. Sit with the notes.
Pass 6 · Proofread
Final polish
Spelling, grammar, the school's name. Read backward to catch typos.
Part 2 · Supplemental essays

The four you'll see most

Why Us? 🎯

Why this school? Name specific programs, professors, clubs — not the rankings.

Community 🏘️

A community you belong to and the role you play in it.

Leadership 🌟

A time you led or made an impact — titles optional, action required.

Diversity 🌍

The perspective or background you'd add to their campus.

Tip: keep a bank of reusable paragraphs — many supplements repeat across schools.

Spotlight · the hardest one

Nailing the "Why Us?" essay

The #1 mistake: an essay that could be copy-pasted to any school. Specificity is everything — prove you did your homework.

❌ Too generic

  • "Great academics and beautiful campus"
  • "Highly ranked and prestigious"
  • Facts from the homepage

✓ Specific & personal

  • A named course, professor, or lab
  • A club or tradition you'd join
  • How it connects to your goals
Part 3 · Recommendation letters

How to ask — the right way

  • Ask early — at least 3–4 weeks before the deadline. Teachers write many.
  • Ask in person first, then follow up in writing with the details.
  • Pick teachers who know you — recent, and from a class where you showed growth.
  • Make it easy — share your resume, deadlines, and a few reminders of your best moments.
  • Say thank you — a note after, and let them know where you got in.
Copy this

Recommender outreach template

Hi [Teacher's name],

I really enjoyed your [class] last year — especially [a specific project or moment]. I'm applying to college this fall and would be honored if you'd write me a letter of recommendation.

My earliest deadline is [date]. If you're able to, I'll send you my resume, a short "brag sheet," and the submission links so it's as easy as possible. Totally understand if your plate is full!

Thank you so much for considering it. — [Your name]
